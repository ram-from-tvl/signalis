# B2B Outreach Copywriting Style Guide

Use this skill whenever drafting or revising the `message_copy` for an
outreach touchpoint. It covers tone by channel, how to reference a buying
signal without sounding surveillance-creepy, how to sequence a multi-touch
cadence, and concrete good/bad examples. It does not cover what fields to
output or how many touchpoints to generate — that's governed by the task
instructions in the calling prompt, not this skill.

## Channel-appropriate tone

- **Email**: Written like a peer emailing a peer, not a broadcast. Short
  paragraphs (1-3 sentences), one clear ask per email, a subject line that
  reads like a real subject a colleague would write (specific, lowercase-ish,
  no clickbait punctuation). No "Dear [Name]," no marketing-department voice.
- **LinkedIn**: More casual than email, assumes the reader is skimming on
  mobile between other things. Open with something that could only be said to
  this person — a shared context, their content, their company's recent
  move — not a pitch. Never open a LinkedIn message with "I noticed you..."
  followed immediately by a feature list; that pattern is the single most
  recognizable "this is templated outreach" tell on the platform.
- **Call script** (for a scheduled call touchpoint, not a cold dial): Written
  as talking points and a real opening line, not a paragraph to be read
  verbatim. Leads with a specific, low-commitment question the rep can
  actually ask out loud, not a value-prop recitation. Assume the first 10
  seconds decide whether the call continues.

## Referencing a buying signal without sounding surveillance-creepy

The signal should motivate *why you're reaching out now*, not prove you've
been watching the prospect's every move. The test: would the prospect feel
informed, or monitored?

- **Weak / creepy**: "I saw you visited our pricing page 3 times this week
  and viewed the enterprise tier for 4 minutes." (Exact counts and durations
  read as surveillance, not relevance.)
- **Strong**: "Since pricing is usually the last thing people dig into before
  a real evaluation starts, I wanted to reach out directly rather than let
  you go through that alone." (States the *inference*, not the raw
  telemetry — the prospect can't tell exactly what was tracked, only that
  the rep drew a reasonable, human conclusion.)
- General rule: translate the signal into the *business moment* it implies
  ("you're comparing options," "your team is evaluating this quarter," "this
  came up because of X") rather than describing the tracked event itself.
  Never mention page-visit counts, time-on-page, or specific timestamps in
  outward-facing copy.

## Structuring a 3-5 touchpoint cadence

- **Touchpoint 1 (day 0-1)**: Low-commitment, signal-motivated, single
  question or single value point. Never asks for a meeting yet if the stage
  is early.
- **Touchpoint 2 (day 3-5)**: Adds one new piece of value (a resource, a
  relevant proof point, a specific outcome a similar company got) rather
  than repeating touchpoint 1's ask in different words. Restating the same
  ask with no new information is the most common cadence mistake.
- **Touchpoint 3 (day 7-10)**: Channel switch is often more effective than a
  third email — e.g. move to LinkedIn or propose a specific call time.
  Directness should increase with stage: early-stage leads still get
  educational framing here; late-stage leads get a concrete next-step ask
  (a specific day/time, not "let me know if you're interested").
- **Touchpoint 4-5 (day 12-16, only for longer/late-stage cadences)**: A
  genuine breakup/close-the-loop message ("I'll stop reaching out unless...")
  performs better than a fourth soft nudge — it gives the prospect low-effort
  permission to either re-engage or opt out, and often produces replies from
  people who were quietly interested but hadn't acted.
- Day offsets should never be evenly spaced by rote (0, 3, 6, 9...) —
  space later touches further apart than early ones; interest that hasn't
  materialized in the first week rarely does from equal-interval pressure.

## Avoiding generic AI-sounding copy

Reject copy with any of these patterns before sending:

- Opens with "I hope this email finds you well" or any weather/pleasantry
  filler.
- Uses "In today's fast-paced business environment," "In the ever-evolving
  landscape of," or any variant — these are the single most recognizable
  AI-outreach tell.
- Lists 3+ generic value props in one message ("increase efficiency, reduce
  costs, and drive growth") instead of one specific, concrete claim.
- Uses "seamlessly," "leverage," "unlock," "empower," "revolutionize,"
  "game-changer," or "cutting-edge" — all now read as synthetic regardless of
  who/what wrote them.
- Ends with a low-commitment non-ask like "Let me know if you have any
  questions!" instead of one specific, easy-to-answer next step ("Worth a
  15-minute call Thursday?").
- Uses the prospect's name and company name more than once each in a short
  message — real humans don't repeat the recipient's own name back to them
  as a rapport tactic.

Good copy instead: one specific, concrete, plausible claim; one clear
signal-informed reason for reaching out now; one easy next step. If a line
could be sent unchanged to any company in any industry, rewrite it — it is
not doing its job.

## Strong vs. weak opening lines

- **Weak**: "Hi {{first_name}}, I hope you're doing well! I wanted to reach
  out because {{company}} could benefit from our industry-leading platform
  that helps businesses like yours drive growth and efficiency."
  (Generic, name-stuffed, no specific reason, three vague value props.)
- **Strong**: "Saw {{company}} is hiring for a second RevOps role this
  quarter — usually a sign the reporting stack is starting to strain at the
  seams." (Specific, references a real signal translated into a business
  inference, gives the reader an obvious reason to keep reading.)
- **Weak**: "Congratulations on the new role! I'd love to tell you about how
  we help companies in your space."
  (Congratulations-as-pretext is a well-known template; "tell you about" is
  seller-centered, not buyer-centered.)
- **Strong**: "Most VPs who take over a team this size end up rebuilding the
  pipeline reporting within the first quarter — is that on your radar yet?"
  (Makes a specific, falsifiable claim about the reader's likely situation
  and asks a real question, rather than announcing an intent to pitch.)

## Tailoring to seniority

- **Individual contributor / manager**: Practical, detail-tolerant, can
  reference specific product mechanics.
- **Director / VP**: Outcome- and team-impact framed; less product detail,
  more "what changes for your function."
- **C-level**: Business-outcome and risk framed only; no product mechanics
  at all; shortest messages of any seniority tier.
